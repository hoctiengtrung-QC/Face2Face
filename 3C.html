• Chúng ta đôi khi tạo tính từ từ danh từ hoặc danh từ từ tính từ bằng cách thêm hậu tố (-ness, -ous, -able, -al, -y, -ity, -ence).

[Take a Break Radio Programme]

PRESENTER: Good afternoon and welcome to Take a Break. Today we're taking a look at the rise in voluntourism around the world. I'm here with Alison Armstrong from the travel website voluntours.net. Alison, first of all, can you tell all our listeners what voluntourism is?

ALISON: Yes, of course. Voluntourism — or volunteer tourism — is when people go on holiday and spend some of their time doing voluntary work in the local community. It could be teaching children, building houses, working on a conservation project — that kind of thing.

PRESENTER: And why is it becoming so popular?

ALISON: Well, I think people want to do something more meaningful with their holidays. A lot of travellers feel that on a normal holiday it's quite hard to meet local people — you stay in a hotel, you visit tourist attractions, but you don't really get under the skin of a place. Voluntourism gives you the chance to really integrate into the local community and make a real difference.

PRESENTER: And who goes on these holidays? Is it mainly young people taking a gap year?

ALISON: That's an interesting question. It used to be mainly young people — maybe 25% of people in the UK take a year off before they're 25. But now voluntourism holidays are for people of all ages. We have families, retired people, professionals — it's really for everyone.

PRESENTER: How much do they cost? Do tourists have to pay to go on these holidays?

ALISON: Yes, the tourists do pay. The fees usually cover accommodation, meals, and project costs. Prices vary a lot, but typically you might pay anywhere from £500 to over £2,000 for a two-week project.

PRESENTER: And do you think voluntourism holidays are always a good idea? Some people are a bit sceptical, aren't they?

ALISON: Yes, you're right. Some critics say that unskilled volunteers can sometimes do more harm than good — or that the money would be better spent hiring local workers. I think that's sometimes true. But if it's done well, voluntourism can be incredibly rewarding for both the volunteer and the local community. The key is to choose a reputable organisation.

PRESENTER: That's really useful. Thank you, Alison.

VOLUNTOURS 🌍
Giving the world a helping hand
HOMEREVIEWSTRAVELOG
Shelley Parker, Australia

I'm on a voluntourism holiday in Nepal and wanted to tell everyone all about it! I've been working at a school in a village near Kathmandu for two weeks and I've enjoyed every minute of it. The best thing about the holiday has been working with the children. I spend six hours a day at the school, mainly helping the teachers in the classroom and organising games for the younger kids. I've also been teaching English to the older children, which is great fun.

I'm amazed at the kindness and patience of the local staff, they've really helped me feel at home. I'm staying with a Nepalese family in the village and I share a room with another volunteer. My bed isn't very comfortable, but I'm usually so exhausted it doesn't matter! On our days off we go walking in the mountains and I'm getting very fit and healthy. The scenery here is absolutely wonderful and I love being surrounded by nature. But it can be a bit dangerous so we always take a guide with us.

The most difficult thing about the holiday is being away from my family – this is the first time I've been out of Australia. I'm glad this type of holiday is becoming more popular with young people and I'd recommend this experience to anyone.

VOLUNTOURS 🌍
Giving the world a helping hand
HOMEREVIEWSTRAVELOG
William Boyd, Scotland

I've been staying on Grenada, an island in the Caribbean, for three weeks and I'm having a brilliant time! There are nine volunteers in our group and everyone's very kind and helpful. We're staying at a campsite by the beach and I share a tent with two other volunteers.

At first I had a lot of difficulty getting to sleep and I missed the comfort of my own bed, but I'm sleeping OK now and I love living in such a natural environment. We take turns to prepare meals for the group, which is the worst part of the holiday for me because I hate cooking. We have one day off a week, which I spend diving around the coral reefs near here.

But of course the main reason we're here is to protect the turtles from other animals and people who want to steal their eggs. We also check each turtle's health and measure how big they are. Turtles have been living on our planet for 230 million years – they're even older than the dinosaurs – but now they're in danger of becoming extinct. You have to be very patient, but it's incredible to watch a turtle walk out of the sea and lay her eggs on the beach. That's definitely been the most amazing part of the holiday for me. I'm not surprised voluntourism is increasing in popularity and I'd definitely recommend coming to Grenada.

IS THIS WHAT HOLIDAYS USED TO BE LIKE?
A family tries Monkton Wyld — an eco-holiday centre in Dorset, England

To be ¹honest, I was a bit nervous about a holiday at Monkton Wyld. 'We all sleep in one room – including the children, the food is vegetarian only and you do your own washing-up?' I asked my wife. 'That's right,' she said. 'Let's do it. It'll be an ²adventure!'

'But we need a holiday,' I said.

Two months later we're on a train to Dorset in south-west England. I'm reading about Monkton on my phone. The house was built in 1848 and since then, it's been a hotel, a boarding school and it's now a farm and a centre for environmental education. It offers ³activity weeks on everything from beekeeping to building your own outdoor toilet!

We're going on a 'Family Week' with our two children, five and eight. It's advertised as "Everything family holidays should be – songs, games, country walks and ⁴homemade meals." Personally, I remember childhood holidays on a beach in Italy, enjoying pizza in a restaurant.

We arrive at suppertime and enjoy the best vegetarian lasagne I've ever tasted – very ⁵popular with the children too. All the vegetables are grown in the garden of the house. Soon I'm feeling a lot happier about the week ahead.

The house is run by 12 people who live permanently at the centre. There are also volunteers who come and stay at the house for a week just to work in the garden or help with the cooking. Some of the families have been coming to Monkton Wyld for several years – it's become a ⁶tradition for them. They're fantastically ⁷patient with all my questions about the place. Later, with the youngest children in bed, we're playing silly games in the large living room. It already feels like these people are old friends.

Over the next week, we really did have a week of 'family fun'. We painted. We sang. We made things. We walked to the beach in nearby Lyme Regis – no pizza though, just chips. And best of all, the children were outside almost the whole time – away from computers, TV and so on. The rooms weren't as ⁸comfortable as a 5-star hotel, of course. But several mornings, we managed to have a lie-in – something that just isn't normally possible on holiday.

Even the washing-up seemed fun. Well, sort of!
